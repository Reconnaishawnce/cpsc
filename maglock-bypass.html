Maglock force bypass

An electromagnetic lock holds a door with nothing but magnetic force. There is no bolt and no keeper, so whether the door holds comes down to how much force the magnet, its mounting, and the frame can resist. Underspecified or poorly mounted maglocks are pulled, shoved, shocked, or levered open, often at the top corner where a little leverage goes a long way, with no power cut and no credential presented.

Attack tool

How the weakness works

An electromagnetic lock is an electromagnet on the frame and a steel armature plate on the door. Energize the magnet and it clamps the plate, holding the door shut. Its rating is a holding force in pounds, commonly 600, 1200, or 1650 lb. Unlike a latch or a deadbolt, it has no mechanical bolt. Nothing physically catches the door. The only thing resisting an opening pull is the magnet.

That is the weakness. Holding force is measured as a straight, dead pull on a perfectly seated plate. In the real world an attacker does not pull straight. They lever the door at a corner, where distance from the hinge multiplies their force, or they shock it with a sharp shove that the rated static number never accounts for. A 600 lb unit on a main door, or a 1200 lb unit on a flexing hollow-metal frame, can come free with surprisingly little effort.

Mounting decides as much as the rating. If the bracket flexes, the armature is bolted with short screws into thin metal, or the frame itself bends, the plate peels away from the magnet well below the rated force. The door opens, no power was cut, and unless a bond sensor is watching, nothing alarms until the door is already swinging.

Field note: A holding-force number on a spec sheet is a best-case dead pull. Treat it as a ceiling, not a guarantee. On a door worth protecting, the durable answer is a lock that throws a real bolt; where a maglock stays, the rating, the brackets, the frame, and a bond monitor all have to be right together.

Mitigation framework
How to defend against this

Four layers, strongest first. A lock that throws a real bolt takes force off the table, right-sizing and rigidly mounting the maglock raises the bar where one stays, and a bond monitor turns a pried door into an alarm. All of it must preserve free egress.

Category:
Latch it, do not just hold it
Hardware

The durable fix is a lock with a real bolt. Force then meets steel in a strike, not a magnet on a bracket. Keep egress free.

Where you can, use an electrified mortise lock, an electric strike, or electric latch retraction on an exit device, so a thrown bolt holds the door rather than magnetism alone.
On egress doors, pair the latching lock with code-compliant free egress (a lever, push bar, or listed release), so people inside always leave in a single motion.
Reserve pure maglocks for doors where a bolt is not practical, and treat holding force as a ceiling, not a guarantee.
Confirm the choice against your egress and fire code first; a maglock is fail-safe and must release on power loss and on fire alarm.
Right-size the holding force
Hardware

Where a maglock stays, match its rating to the door and the threat, and buy the listing, not the sticker number.

Specify a high holding force (1200 to 1650 lb) on perimeter and secured doors, not a 600 lb unit meant for an interior office door.
Prefer a UL 294 listed lock from a known maker, and read the rating as a best-case straight pull, not a real-world pry.
On pairs of doors and wide leaves, use a unit per leaf and account for the leverage a tall door gives an attacker at the far corner.
Choose a lock with a built-in bond and door-position monitor so the rating is backed by a way to know it is actually holding.
Mount it so it cannot be pried loose
Physical

A strong magnet on a weak bracket is a weak lock. Most real-world failures are the mounting, not the magnet.

Through-bolt or sex-bolt the magnet and the armature rather than relying on short screws into thin metal.
Use rigid factory brackets (L, Z, or header) and reinforce hollow-metal frames so the header cannot flex under a pull.
Mount the armature with its intended slight play and anti-rotation so it seats flat and reaches full bond, not a partial one.
Place and shield the hardware so it cannot be reached or unbolted from the insecure side, and monitor its tamper output.
Monitor the bond and alarm on force
Detection

A maglock that cannot tell you it has been pried is a silent one. Make a forced opening loud in real time.

Use the magnetic bond sensor (MBS) and door-position contact, and alarm the instant bond is lost without a valid release.
Keep the door-forced shunt short, since an attacker is through the moment the plate separates.
Alarm on a door-open with no matching badge or request-to-exit, so a forced opening never clears as a normal exit.
Cover the door with a camera and route any unexpected after-hours opening to the monitoring center to investigate.
