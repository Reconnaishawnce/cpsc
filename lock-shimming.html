Spring latch shimming

The beveled spring latch — standard on most commercial and residential locks — can be retracted using nothing more than a credit card, hotel key card, or thin piece of plastic. No key, no tool kit, no expertise. It's one of the oldest bypass techniques in existence and remains devastatingly effective.

Attack tool
Credit card / plastic strip ($0)
Time to execute
5–30 seconds
Skill required
Minimal to moderate
Detection risk
Very low (quiet, no damage)
Alert generated
None if door contact not armed

How the weakness works

The standard spring latch bolt — found in virtually every lockset from $20 residential hardware to commercial cylindrical locks — has an angled face (the bevel) so that the door can close and latch without requiring the user to turn the knob. When the door closes, the angled face slides against the strike plate and retracts automatically.

That bevel is the vulnerability. Any thin, flexible object inserted between the door edge and the door frame at the latch point can replicate what the strike plate does: push the angled face inward, retracting the bolt, and allowing the door to swing open. Credit cards, hotel key cards, loyalty cards, thin plastic rulers, and a dozen other everyday objects work reliably.

On doors where the latch is accessible from the outside — meaning the frame doesn't have a lip concealing the latch face — a skilled attacker can open the door in under five seconds. On doors with better strike geometry, it may take 30 seconds and some wiggling, but the technique still works.

01Attacker identifies a door secured only by a spring latch (no deadbolt engaged, no auxiliary latch pin). The latch face may be visible in the gap between door and frame.
02Shim is inserted into the gap at the latch location and angled toward the latch bevel. Card is worked in a rocking/pulling motion to engage the angled face of the bolt.
03Shim pushes the latch bevel inward, retracting the bolt out of the strike plate. Latch is now fully retracted — door no longer held.
04Door opens. No alarm fires unless a door contact is monitored. The access control system records nothing — no credential was ever read. Entry appears as a normal door-open event if logged at all.
Field note: Any door with a spring latch, a visible latch gap, and no deadbolt is shimable. Hotels are the canonical environment — but corporate interior doors, server room perimeters, and stairwell entries regularly exhibit this vulnerability. The auxiliary deadlocking plunger (found on better locksets) prevents shimming when the door is closed properly.
How it happens Latch secured
The spring latch is extended into the strike plate, holding the door closed. The angled bevel face is visible at the gap between door edge and frame — the attack surface.
Mitigation framework
How to defend against this

Deadbolts are the most reliable fix. Anti-shim latches and frame armor close the gap for doors where a deadbolt isn't practical.

Category:
Deadbolt enforcement
Hardware upgrade

A deadbolt eliminates the bevel entirely — its bolt is square-faced and cannot be retracted by a shim. The single most effective control against latch shimming.

ANSI Grade 1 single-cylinder deadbolt for all high-security interior doors
Double-cylinder deadbolt (keyed both sides) where exterior-accessible glass panels exist near the lock — prevents reachover attacks
High-security cylinders (Medeco, Mul-T-Lock, ASSA) resist picking and bumping in addition to shimming
Electrified deadbolts for access-controlled doors — credential on exterior, deadbolt engagement on interior
Anti-shim latch hardware
Hardware upgrade

Modern commercial locksets include an auxiliary deadlocking plunger that automatically deadlocks the latch when the door is closed — preventing shimming without requiring a separate deadbolt.

ANSI Grade 1 cylindrical and mortise locks include a deadlocking plunger by specification — verify it's engaged when the door closes properly
Anti-shim latch guards (surface applied to the door edge) block card insertion at the latch point
Security strike plates with a wrap-around lip cover the latch face from the frame side — shim cannot reach the bevel
Mortise locks provide a more robust latch mechanism and typically include a deadlocking feature
Door frame hardening
Physical

Reduce or eliminate the gap between door edge and frame where the shim is inserted. Close tolerances make shimming difficult or impossible.

Rebated (rabbeted) door frames wrap around the door edge — there is no accessible gap at the latch location
Door frame armor (Don-Jo, Armor Concepts) plates the gap between door and frame with steel, blocking shim insertion
Wrap-around strike plates extend across the frame face and into the gap, eliminating shim access to the latch bevel
Latch shields and door edge guards can be added to existing frames without replacing hardware
Operational policy
Policy

Where hardware cannot be immediately changed, operational controls reduce risk and increase detection probability.

Enforce deadbolt engagement policy — require employees to fully engage deadbolts when leaving secure areas
Audit doors without deadbolts and prioritize for hardware upgrade; intermediate risk-acceptance documentation
Include spring latch awareness in security training — staff report doors that don't close and latch properly
Regular physical security walk-throughs to identify doors with visible latch gaps or misaligned strikes
