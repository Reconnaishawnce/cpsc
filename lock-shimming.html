Spring latch shimming

The beveled spring latch found on most commercial and residential locks can be pushed back with a credit card, a hotel key card, or any thin strip of plastic. No key and no specialized tools are involved. It is one of the oldest bypass techniques there is, and on any door that relies on the latch alone it still works. The controls that stop it are well understood, and this page walks through them.

Attack tool
Credit card / plastic strip ($0)
Time to execute
5–30 seconds
Skill required
Minimal to moderate
Detection risk
Very low (quiet, no damage)
Alert generated
None if door contact not armed

How the weakness works

The standard spring latch bolt, found in virtually every lockset from inexpensive residential hardware to commercial cylindrical locks, has an angled face (the bevel) so that the door can close and latch without the user turning the knob. When the door closes, the angled face slides against the strike plate and retracts automatically.

That bevel is the vulnerability. Any thin, flexible object inserted between the door edge and the door frame at the latch point can replicate what the strike plate does: push the angled face inward, retracting the bolt, and allowing the door to swing open. Credit cards, hotel key cards, loyalty cards, thin plastic rulers, and a dozen other everyday objects work reliably.

On doors where the latch is accessible from the outside, meaning the frame doesn't have a lip concealing the latch face, an attacker can open the door in seconds. On doors with better strike geometry it takes longer and some wiggling, but the technique still works.

01Attacker identifies a door secured only by a spring latch (no deadbolt engaged, no auxiliary latch pin). The latch face may be visible in the gap between door and frame.
02Shim is inserted into the gap at the latch location and angled toward the latch bevel. Card is worked in a rocking/pulling motion to engage the angled face of the bolt.
03Shim pushes the latch bevel inward, retracting the bolt out of the strike plate. Latch is now fully retracted, and the door is no longer held.
04Door opens. No alarm fires unless a door contact is monitored. The access control system records nothing, because no credential was ever read. Entry appears as a normal door-open event if logged at all.
Field note: Any door with a spring latch, a visible latch gap, and no deadbolt is shimable. Hotels are the canonical environment, but corporate interior doors, server room perimeters, and stairwell entries regularly show the same weakness. The auxiliary deadlocking plunger (found on better locksets) prevents shimming when the door is closed properly.
How it happens Latch secured
The spring latch is extended into the strike plate, holding the door closed. The angled bevel face is visible at the gap between door edge and frame, which is the attack surface.
Mitigation framework
How to defend against this

Five layers. Start by auditing the doors to find which ones are weak, then fix them strongest first: a deadbolt or a deadlocking latch removes the bevel the card works against, closing the door-to-frame gap denies the shim its path, and operational habits keep that hardware actually engaged.

Category:
Run a door audit
Operational

Before you can fix a weak door you have to find it. A recurring walk-through surfaces the doors that no longer close, latch, or alarm the way they should, and gets them onto a fix list.

Walk every door on a set cadence, every 6 to 12 months, with facilities, your integrator, a locksmith, and security together so each trade catches what it knows best.
Let each door close on its own from several distances and watch how it latches. A door that does not close and latch fully is the one that gets shimmed.
Check the door-forced and door-held-open alarms (DFO/DHO), the strike plate and its screws, the hinges, the closer, and any catches or holders.
Fix the quick items on the spot, ticket the rest so a weak door is tracked to closure, and use the door audit guide for the full checklist.
Add a deadbolt
Hardware upgrade

A deadbolt is the durable fix. Its bolt is square-faced with no spring bevel, so a card in the gap has nothing to push against. Strongest options first.

Fit an ANSI/BHMA Grade 1 single-cylinder deadbolt on doors that protect anything worth protecting, and require that it be thrown, not just pulled shut on the latch.
Pair the deadbolt with a high-security cylinder (Medeco, Mul-T-Lock, ASSA) so an attacker cannot simply fall back to picking or bumping the lock instead.
On access-controlled openings, use an electrified deadbolt so the bolt itself releases on a valid credential rather than leaving a shimmable spring latch as the only thing holding the door.
Where glass or a sidelite sits within arm's reach of the thumbturn, specify a double-cylinder deadbolt so a reach-through cannot simply turn it open, subject to your egress code.
Use a deadlocking latch
Hardware upgrade

Where a separate deadbolt is not practical, a deadlatch closes the same gap: a small plunger beside the main bolt locks it against retraction once the door is shut.

Specify a Grade 1 cylindrical or mortise lock with an auxiliary deadlocking plunger, which resists the shim without a second lock to operate.
Confirm the plunger actually lands on the strike, not in the strike hole, when the door closes. A misaligned strike leaves the main bolt free to be shimmed.
Prefer a mortise lock where the door sees heavy use. Its latch mechanism is more robust and the deadlocking feature is standard.
Verify alignment after any door, closer, or hinge adjustment, since a door that no longer closes fully drops the deadlatch back to an ordinary spring latch.
Close the gap
Physical

No accessible gap at the latch means no way to slide a card in against the bevel. This is the fix for existing hardware you are not ready to replace.

Add a wrap-around latch guard (Don-Jo LP or ULP series) that plates over the door-to-frame gap at the latch and blocks a card from ever reaching the bevel.
Reinforce the opening with a frame and strike kit (Armor Concepts, Don-Jo) so the gap stays tight and the door resists the spreading or kick that would open it up.
Where the frame is being replaced anyway, specify a rabbeted frame whose lip wraps the door edge, leaving no accessible gap at the latch at all.
Fit a security strike with a deep, boxed lip so the latch face is shielded from the frame side and cannot be reached from the gap.
Enforce use of hardware
Policy

The best lock is defeated by a habit of not throwing it. Operational controls keep the hardware doing its job and surface doors that have quietly gone weak.

Require staff to throw the deadbolt, not just pull the door shut, when they leave a secured area, and make that the expectation in writing.
Audit the estate for doors held by a spring latch alone, prioritize them for a deadbolt or deadlatch, and document any risk you knowingly accept in the interim.
Fold spring-latch awareness into security training so staff report doors that do not close and latch fully instead of walking past them.
Walk the doors on a set cadence to catch visible latch gaps, misaligned strikes, and closers that no longer pull the door all the way home.
