Door audits

Most of the weaknesses in this library live on doors that were fine the day they were installed and have quietly drifted since. A closer that no longer pulls the door home, a strike screw backed out, a sweep set too high. A door audit is the recurring walk-through that finds those doors before an attacker does, and it is one of the cheapest controls you can run. This guide keeps it simple: which doors, who walks them, and exactly what to check.

Cadence
Every 6 to 12 months
Who
Facilities · Integrator · Locksmith · Security
Time per door
2 to 5 minutes
Output
A prioritized fix list

Labor and a clipboard

Why a walk-through works

A door is a system: a leaf, a frame, a hinge set, a lock or exit device, a closer, a strike, seals at the perimeter, and often a reader and an alarm point. It only holds when every part is doing its job. Any one of them can loosen, sag, or fall out of adjustment without anyone noticing, because the door still looks shut.

Walking the doors on a schedule does three things at once. It prevents, because you catch the loose strike or the high sweep and fix it before it matters. It detects, because you confirm the door-forced and door-held-open alarms actually fire and report. And it lets you proactively fix, turning a vague sense that "some doors are bad" into a specific, ticketed list with owners and dates.

The point is not paperwork. It is to physically stand at each door, let it close on its own, and watch what it does. The doors that fail that simple test are the ones that get shimmed, propped, or worked with an under-door tool.

Field note: The single most useful thing you can do at any door is let it close by itself from a few different starting angles and watch whether it latches every time. A door that latches only when you push it is a door that is not latching.
The audit loop Repeat 6 to 12 mo
01Scope. Pull the door schedule and pick the run: perimeter and secured doors first.
02Walk. Visit each door as a team and work the checklist below, category by category.
03Fix now. Tighten screws, adjust the closer, reset the sweep on the spot where you can.
04Ticket. Log everything else with a photo, a priority, and an owner.
05Close out. Track the tickets to done, then set the date for the next run.
A door audit is a loop, not a one-time project. The value compounds when it runs on a schedule and every finding is tracked to closure.
Scope
Which doors to audit

You do not have to do every door in one pass. Work in priority order so the openings that matter most are covered first and often.

Tier 1: perimeter and high-value
First

The doors an outsider reaches first, and the doors behind which the worst outcome sits. Audit these every time, most often.

Exterior and perimeter doors, including after-hours and loading-dock entrances.
Server rooms, IDF and MDF closets, and other doors protecting critical systems.
Cash handling, evidence, pharmacy, records, and any regulated or high-value room.
Doors that separate public areas from staff-only or secured space.
Tier 2: secured interior
Next

Access-controlled and life-safety doors inside the building. Audit on the standard cadence.

Any door on the access-control system, badged or with a REX device.
Stairwell and cross-corridor doors, especially fire and smoke doors.
Egress doors with panic or exit hardware.
Doors shared with tenants, vendors, or contractors.
Tier 3: general interior
Sample

Lower-risk interior doors. Spot-check a rotating sample rather than every one, every time.

Standard office, meeting, and utility doors without special protection.
Doors flagged by staff tickets, incident reports, or a prior audit finding.
Any door recently rekeyed, rehung, or worked on by a contractor.
A random sample each cycle so problems in this tier still surface.
The team
Who should be in the room

A door audit is better with more than one trade present, because each one sees a different failure. Pull the group together for the walk rather than sending one person with a form.

Facilities
Owner

Owns the door schedule and the building. Usually runs the audit and holds the fix list.

Provides the door schedule, keys, and access to every space.
Judges closers, hinges, thresholds, and general condition of the leaf and frame.
Owns the tickets and the budget for repairs and replacements.
Integrator
Electronics

Your access-control and alarm vendor, or the in-house team that runs those systems.

Confirms readers, REX devices, strikes, and maglocks work and are wired fail-safe or fail-secure as intended.
Tests that door-forced and door-held-open alarms actually fire and reach the monitoring point.
Checks that the access system logs match what the door is doing.
Locksmith
Hardware

Reads the mechanical hardware the way an attacker would, and can fix a lot of it on the spot.

Judges the lockset, latch, deadbolt, deadlatch plunger, and strike for wear and correct fit.
Spots shimmable latches, loose cylinders, and worn or misaligned strikes.
Carries the tools to adjust strikes, tighten hardware, and rekey where needed.
Security
Operations

Knows how the doors are actually used, and what the guard force and cameras can see.

Flags doors that get propped, tailgated, or worked around in daily use.
Confirms camera coverage and lighting at each opening, and that alarms route somewhere staffed.
Sets the priority of each finding against the real threat to that space.
The walk-through
The checklist, by category

At each door, work these categories in order. Not every item applies to every door, but the headings make sure nothing is skipped. Note what fails, fix what you can, and ticket the rest.

1. Operation and closing
Physical

Start here. A door that does not close and latch on its own undoes almost everything else.

Release the door from several angles and distances and confirm it closes fully every time.
Confirm the latch or deadlatch seats in the strike on its own, without a push or a lift.
Check the closer speed and force: it should close and latch but not slam or stall partway.
Watch for a door that binds, drags on the floor, or springs back off the latch.
2. Latch and lock
Hardware

The bolt that actually holds the door. Look for anything a card or a pull can defeat. See CPSC-003.

Identify what holds the door: spring latch, deadlatch with a plunger, deadbolt, or exit device.
Confirm the auxiliary deadlatch plunger lands on the strike face, not in the strike hole.
Where a deadbolt exists, confirm it is actually being thrown, not just the latch.
Check the cylinder is tight and the lever or handle returns fully to position.
3. Gaps and seals
Physical

The clearances an under-door or through-gap tool needs. See CPSC-002.

Check the undercut at the door bottom. A wide, flat gap is the path for an under-door tool.
Confirm sweeps and automatic door bottoms actually contact the threshold and are not set too high.
Check the gap at the latch edge and, on pairs, the astragal at the meeting stile.
Look for daylight around the perimeter and for weatherstripping that has worn away.
4. Strike and frame
Hardware

What the bolt lands in. A loose or worn strike quietly turns a good lock into a weak one.

Confirm the strike is aligned so the bolt and any deadlatch plunger seat correctly.
Tighten strike screws and, on important doors, use long screws that reach the framing.
Check the frame for spread, dents, or prying near the strike and hinge sides.
Consider a latch guard or reinforced strike where the door-to-frame gap is exposed.
5. Hinges and pins
Physical

The other edge of the door. On out-swing doors the hinges are exposed and worth a look.

Check hinge screws are tight and the door does not sag, which throws off the latch.
On out-swing doors, confirm non-removable pins or a hinge stud so the door cannot be lifted.
Look for worn or squeaking hinges that signal a door dropping out of alignment.
Confirm continuous or heavy-duty hinges on high-traffic and high-value doors.
6. Alarms and monitoring
Detection

Prove the door reports. Test the alarms rather than assuming they still work.

Test the door-forced-open (DFO) alarm: open against the lock and confirm it fires and reports.
Test the door-held-open (DHO) alarm: prop the door past the shunt and confirm it trips.
Confirm the door-position switch and any REX device are working and correctly mapped.
Check that alarms route to a place that is actually staffed and that the shunt time is sane.
7. Readers and credentials
Hardware

The electronic side of an access-controlled door. Confirm it fails the way you intend.

Confirm the reader reads a valid credential and rejects an invalid one.
Verify the lock fails secure or fails safe on power loss as the life-safety design requires.
Check the reader and wiring are not loose, exposed, or reachable from the unsecured side.
Confirm access logs match reality and that stale credentials are being pruned.
8. Camera and lighting
Detection

Whether an attempt at this door can be seen and reviewed. Stand where the camera stands.

Confirm a camera covers the door and that the door and floor gap are actually in frame.
Check the opening is lit well enough for that camera at the hours it matters.
Look for blind spots an attacker could use to work the door unseen.
Confirm footage is retained long enough to be useful after the fact.
Close the loop
After the walk-through

An audit only pays off if the findings turn into fixes. Keep the follow-through as simple as the walk.

Fix, ticket, track
Operational
Fix the quick wins during the walk: tighten screws, adjust the closer, reset a sweep, throw a deadbolt back into use.
Ticket everything else with a photo, the door ID, the category, and a priority tied to the space it protects.
Give every ticket an owner and a due date, and track it to closure instead of to a report that gets filed.
Re-test the fix at the next visit so a repair that did not hold is caught.
Make it a program
Operational
Set the next date before you leave: every 6 months for Tier 1, up to 12 for the rest.
Trigger an off-cycle check when a door is rehung, rekeyed, or worked on by a contractor.
Give staff an easy way to report a door that does not close or latch, and feed it into the list.
Trend the findings over time so recurring problems point you at a design or product fix.
