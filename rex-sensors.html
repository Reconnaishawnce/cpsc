Choosing a request-to-exit sensor that resists attack

A request-to-exit sensor unlocks a secured door for anyone leaving. That is a life-safety necessity and a security liability at the same time. This is the long version: how each detection technology actually works, which ones a can of compressed air can defeat, what the codes force you to allow, and how to specify a sensor that holds up. No device is unbeatable, so the aim is to make the cheap attacks fail and the expensive ones loud.

The problem

A plain PIR motion sensor releases the lock on a burst of cold gas, with no person present and no alarm.

The fix

A mechanical exit switch, or a dual-technology, microwave, or active-IR sensor, cannot be triggered by heat alone.

The constraint

Life-safety code requires the door to release for people leaving, so you harden the trigger, not the exit.

The role
What a REX sensor is actually for

On an electronically locked door, people on the inside still have to get out instantly, without a badge, in a fire or a power failure. The request-to-exit (REX) device is what grants that exit. On a door held by a magnetic lock it usually releases the lock as someone approaches. On a door with an electric strike or an exit device, egress is already mechanical, so the REX often just shunts the door-forced alarm while the person pushes through.

That distinction matters more than any spec sheet. A REX that only shunts an alarm cannot let an attacker in, because the latch is still mechanical. A REX that unlocks the door is a remote release, and anything that can trigger it from the wrong side is a way in. The compressed-gas attack (CPSC-001) works precisely because the most common REX sensor, a passive-infrared motion detector, will release a maglock for a plume of cold air.

So the goal of this page is narrow and practical: keep the exit reliable for the people who belong inside, and make the release impossible to fake from outside. Everything below serves that.

Rule of thumb

The safest posture is to let the lock be released by free mechanical egress and the code-required fail-safe path, while the electronic REX only shunts the alarm and logs the event. Reserve unlock-on-motion for doors that truly need it, and there use the least forgeable sensor you can. A maglock whose only inside release is a motion sensor is the configuration most worth eliminating.

What the codes require
Why the door has to let go

The reason a spoof is dangerous is that the law requires the lock to fail open for people leaving. You cannot engineer that away; you engineer the trigger instead.

A locked egress door in the United States lives under building and life-safety code, and those codes put getting people out ahead of keeping people out. Two things they require are exactly what the compressed-gas attack abuses: the lock must unlock on loss of power, and, where an approach sensor is used, the door must release when it detects someone leaving. A sensor that faithfully obeys an exit request will faithfully obey a forged one. The fix is never to defeat those requirements. It is to choose a trigger that is hard to forge, put it out of reach, and verify every opening.

The compliant way to harden

Prefer door-hardware release (IBC 1010.2.11 / NFPA 7.2.1.6.3), where a hand physically operates the hardware, over sensor release (IBC 1010.2.12 / NFPA 7.2.1.6.2) wherever the occupancy and the authority having jurisdiction allow. None of the hardening on this page may compromise the required fail-safe egress: a lock that would trap occupants to defeat a spoof is not code-compliant. Confirm the exact section and edition your AHJ has adopted.
