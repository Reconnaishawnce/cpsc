Under-door lever manipulation

A purpose-built tool, or even a bent wire coat hanger, slides through the gap at the base of a door and hooks the interior lever handle. The attacker pulls down. The door opens. The access control system logs nothing, because no credential was ever read.

Attack tool
Loider / wire / film (~$0–80)
Time to execute
5–15 seconds
Skill required
Minimal
Detection risk
Low (no event generated)
Alert generated
Door forced alarm only

How the weakness works

Most commercial and office doors are secured with an electronic access control system on the exterior (a card reader or keypad), while the interior side has a simple lever handle or push bar that anyone inside can use to exit. This design is correct for life safety: people always need to be able to exit freely.

The vulnerability is the door gap. Standard doors have a gap of ½ to 1 inch at the bottom. A thin, rigid tool, whether purpose-built or improvised, can pass through that gap, extend upward inside the secured space, hook around the lever handle, and depress it. The latch retracts. The door opens.

No credential is ever presented, so the access control system has nothing to log. If the door has a forced-door alarm (monitoring the door contact), it may fire, but only after the latch is already depressed and the door begins to open. In practice, many door contacts are set with a delay or are ignored after hours.

01Attacker confirms the door uses a lever handle or push bar on the interior, and has a visible gap at the base. Most hollow metal frames in commercial buildings qualify.
02Tool is inserted flat through the door gap. Purpose-built "loid" tools use a flexible stem with a hook end. Improvised versions use wire or a coat hanger bent to shape.
03Tool is positioned against the interior lever handle and rotated or pulled downward. The lever depresses, retracting the latch bolt.
04Door swings open from the outside. No badge event, no REX event. Only a door-open alarm fires, and only if one is configured and monitored.
Field note: This technique works on the majority of commercial doors in US corporate environments. The only reliable real-time detection is camera coverage with alert logic. Recessed lever handles and door bottoms significantly slow the attack but rarely eliminate it entirely.

Mitigation framework
How to defend against this

Five layers, strongest first. Removing the exposed lever ends the attack, sealing the gap denies the tool its path, a person watching the door stops an attempt as it happens, and the rest narrows the window and makes an attempt visible.

Category:
Remove the lever from the attack
Hardware

The durable fix is to leave nothing under the door worth hooking. Strongest options first.

Fit a push-bar exit device. A horizontal push pad has no lever or knob to catch, so an under-door tool has no target.
Concealed vertical rod hardware routes the latching inside the door leaf and latches top and bottom, so there is no exposed mechanism to reach at all.
Where the door stays access-controlled, use electric latch retraction or a fail-secure electric strike so it releases on a credential, not on any interior handle.
If a lever must remain for code or accessibility reasons, pair it with the gap and detection controls below rather than relying on it alone.
Close the gap
Physical

No flat clearance under the door means no way to slide a tool in and up to the hardware.

Add an automatic door bottom that drops a continuous seal onto the threshold when the door closes, taking the undercut to near zero.
For a lighter retrofit, a compression door sweep closes the same clearance against the floor or threshold.
Where the opening is being renovated anyway, replacing the door and frame as a set lets you spec a tight undercut and matched threshold from the start, so the gap the tool needs is never built in.
Combine with a threshold, and on pairs of doors an astragal at the meeting stile, so there is no path in from any edge.
Verify the finished gap in the field. A sweep set too high leaves exactly the clearance the tool needs.
Guard the lever handle
Physical

If the interior hardware has to stay a lever, make it hard to reach and hard to hook.

Add an interior lever shroud or guard that blocks a tool from catching the handle and pulling it down.
Choose hardware where the lever returns nearly flush and offers little edge for a hook to grab.
Reposition the hardware away from the door edge and the undercut so an angled tool cannot line up on it.
Fit a floor-level guard plate on the interior across the undercut next to the hardware.
Put a person on the door
Personnel

The attack needs a tool worked slowly at the floor in front of the door. Someone watching that spot both prevents the attempt and ends it in the moment, not on later review.

Station a guard with a direct line of sight to the door during the hours it is exposed. A person kneeling to feed a tool under the door cannot do so unnoticed, so the attempt is stopped before the latch ever moves.
Unlike a camera, an officer on post intervenes in real time: they can challenge the person, hold the door, and call it in while the attempt is still underway rather than after entry.
Where a fixed post is not justified, fold the door into a guard tour or reception sightline so it is checked on a known cadence and is never left unwatched for long stretches.
Brief officers on exactly what this attempt looks like, a thin tool worked at the base of the door, so it is recognized as an attack and not mistaken for a dropped keycard or maintenance.
Alarm triggers for UDT
Detection

Where prevention is not yet in place, make an attempt loud and reviewable in real time.

Set the forced-door alarm to zero or minimal delay. The attacker is inside the moment the door swings, so a long shunt hands them the room.
Cover the door at floor level with a camera framed to catch a tool at the gap, and alarm on motion at the door perimeter after hours.
Monitor the door-position contact and route any unexpected after-hours opening to the monitoring center as an event to investigate.
Alarm on a door-open with no matching badge or request-to-exit, so a credential-free opening never clears silently.
